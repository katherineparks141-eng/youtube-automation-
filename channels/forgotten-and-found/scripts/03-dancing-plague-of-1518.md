# The Dancing Plague of 1518: Strasbourg's Real Dance-Till-You-Die Mystery
Channel: Forgotten & Found | Pillar: Strange But True (SB) | Target length: 9–10 min

---

**[0:00–0:20] COLD OPEN**

(VO, measured, drawn in — not sensational)

In the summer of 1518, a woman in the city of Strasbourg stepped into the street outside her home and began to dance. She didn't stop that day. She didn't stop the next day either. Within a week, thirty more people were dancing beside her, on feet already swollen and bleeding. Within a month, it was closer to four hundred.

Nobody had played a single note of music.

**[0:20–0:50] THE PROMISE**

By the end of this video, you'll know exactly what the surviving physician notes, cathedral sermons, and city council records actually say happened in Strasbourg that summer, what the city's own doctors tried to do about it, and which of the three competing modern theories — poisoning, mass panic, or religious fervor — the evidence actually supports best.

**[0:50–2:15] FRAU TROFFEA STEPS INTO THE STREET**

(Visual: cobblestone street, half-timbered Strasbourg houses, 1518 styling; a lone woman dancing)

On July 14, 1518, a woman the records name as Frau Troffea walked out of her house in Strasbourg — then a free city of the Holy Roman Empire, in what's now eastern France — and began to dance. Not briefly. She kept going for somewhere between four and six days straight, pausing only when her body gave out from exhaustion, by most accounts dancing until her feet bled inside her shoes.

Her husband reportedly begged her to stop. She didn't, or couldn't.

This wasn't the first time dancing mania had been recorded in medieval Europe — smaller outbreaks go back centuries, usually clustered around the feast days of Saint Vitus and Saint John. But what happened next in Strasbourg is the best-documented case there's ever been, because six separate chronicle accounts, physician notes, and city council records all survived.

**[2:15–3:30] IT SPREADS**

(Visual: growing crowd of dancers filling a town square, exhausted, barefoot)

Within about a week, more than thirty people had joined Troffea, dancing in the streets with no music and, by most accounts, no apparent desire to stop. Within a month, estimates in the surviving records run as high as four hundred people.

The physical toll was severe and specific: convulsive, spasmodic movement; dancers collapsing from heart attacks, hunger, or sheer exhaustion; feet bleeding inside shoes soaked through with sweat and blood. Some later retellings claim as many as fifteen people a day died at the outbreak's peak — though it's worth being direct about this: Strasbourg's own contemporary sources don't actually record a death toll, and that specific figure traces mainly to later historians, not the original city records. What the original sources agree on is that the physical suffering was real, visible, and alarming enough that the city government got involved almost immediately.

**[3:30–5:00] WHAT THE CITY ACTUALLY TRIED**

(Visual: Strasbourg city council chamber; physicians examining a dancer)

Strasbourg's city council first treated this as a medical problem. They brought in physicians, who diagnosed it as "overheated blood" — and prescribed, remarkably, more dancing: the idea was that the afflicted needed to dance the sickness out of their systems. Acting on that advice, the council cleared out guild halls, built a temporary wooden stage, and hired musicians and strong assistants to help keep the dancers upright and moving.

By every surviving account, this made things dramatically worse. The music and the open space seems to have drawn in still more dancers rather than curing anyone.

So the council reversed course entirely: public dancing and music were banned outright, and the dancers were instead taken to the shrine of Saint Vitus, patron saint associated with this specific affliction. There, in a ritual combining folk belief and formal church practice, they wore red shoes sprinkled with holy water, carried small wooden crosses, and underwent prayers and incense rites conducted in Latin. By early September, the remaining dancers were led to a chapel on a nearby mountain to pray for absolution — and, according to the records, the outbreak finally ended there.

**[5:00–7:30] THE THREE THEORIES — RANKED FROM WEAKEST TO STRONGEST**

(Visual: three-panel comparison graphic — mold on rye grain, a crowded medieval street, a saint's shrine)

Modern historians have spent nearly a century arguing over what actually caused this, and the evidence doesn't point to one clean answer. Here's where it stands, from weakest to strongest.

Third, and weakest: ergot poisoning. Ergot is a fungus that grows on damp rye and produces ergotamine, a compound chemically related to LSD, capable of causing hallucinations, muscle spasms, and agitation — which sounds, on the surface, like a tidy explanation. The problem, raised directly by historian John Waller in a 2009 Lancet article, is physiological: ergotism is a severe, often gangrenous poisoning that would be far more likely to kill or incapacitate someone than let them dance continuously for days, and outbreaks of dancing mania have been recorded in regions and seasons where rye-based ergot contamination doesn't fit the pattern. Strasbourg's surviving records also don't mention the blackened, gangrenous symptoms ergotism typically produces.

Second: religious and supernatural causes, as Strasbourg's own residents understood it at the time. To the sixteenth-century mind, this was straightforwardly a matter of divine punishment or saintly curse — specifically tied to Saint Vitus and Saint John, whose feast days were already associated with earlier, smaller dancing outbreaks across medieval Europe. This explains why the city's actual solution was religious rather than medical: the pilgrimage, the red shoes, the Latin rites. As a description of how people at the time made sense of the event, this theory is on solid ground. As a scientific explanation for why it physically happened, it isn't one historians treat as literal causation today.

First, and the theory with the most scholarly support currently: mass psychogenic illness, sometimes called mass hysteria — a stress-induced condition that can spread through a community via observation and social contagion, without any toxin or pathogen involved at all. The timing fits unusually well: the summer of 1518 followed years of famine, a severe outbreak of bubonic plague, catastrophic flooding, and an unusually brutal heat wave — the kind of sustained, compounding social and physical stress that researchers have documented as a trigger for exactly this kind of collective psychogenic response in other historical cases. Historian John Waller, who has written the most extensive modern study of this outbreak, argues the combination of extreme stress and genuine local belief in dancing curses created the psychological conditions for it to start — and then spread, dancer to dancer, as others in a traumatized, superstitious population responded the same way. More recent scholars studying the event, including Lynneth Miller Renberg and Kathryn Dickason, broadly support this reading while also stressing that the strict "what caused it" framing can obscure what the event actually meant to the terrified people living through it.

**[7:30–8:45] WHAT'S STILL UNRESOLVED**

(Visual: present-day Strasbourg cathedral and old town, calm daylight)

Here's the honest caveat: nobody — not Waller, not any historian working on this since — claims certainty. The six surviving chronicle accounts don't fully agree with each other on basic details, including whether the first dancer was really named Troffea at all. The actual death toll is unknown; it could be as low as zero directly attributable deaths, or, if later claims about daily fatalities hold up, it could run into the hundreds. No modern researcher has been able to close that gap using the surviving sources alone.

What isn't in dispute is that something real, visible, and physically dangerous happened in Strasbourg's streets for roughly a month in the summer of 1518, severe enough that a city government organized an official, costly response to it — twice, after the first attempt failed.

**[8:45–9:30] CLOSE / CTA**

Five hundred years later, the strongest explanation historians have is still essentially: a city, already broken by years of famine, plague, and disaster, found itself afraid enough, together, that hundreds of people's bodies did something none of them could consciously control. That's a stranger and more human answer than demons or poison — and it's the one the evidence actually supports.
