# Port Royal: The Pirate City the Sea Swallowed in Two Minutes
Channel: Forgotten & Found | Pillar: Lost Places (LP) | Target length: 9–10 min

---

**[0:00–0:20] COLD OPEN**

(VO, measured, drawn in — not sensational)

On June 7th, 1693 — sorry, 1692 — at thirty-eight minutes past eleven in the morning, a third of a city dropped straight into the sea. Not over years. Not over days. In under two minutes, warehouses, forts, and entire streets of Port Royal, Jamaica slid off a sand spit and into the harbor, with people still standing in them.

Divers can still find it down there. Cobblestone streets, brick foundations, even cutlery, sitting on the harbor floor exactly where the ground gave out.

**[0:20–0:50] THE PROMISE**

By the end of this video, you'll know what Port Royal actually was before the earthquake — and it's wilder than "pirate town" — what the eyewitness accounts from that single morning describe, and what nautical archaeologists have actually recovered from the submerged half of the city in the centuries since.

**[0:50–2:30] "THE WICKEDEST CITY ON EARTH"**

(Visual: a crowded 17th-century harbor town, tall ships at anchor, taverns spilling light onto a narrow street at dusk)

Port Royal sat at the tip of a narrow sand spit guarding the entrance to Kingston Harbour, and by the 1680s it was, by most estimates, one of the largest and wealthiest English settlements in the Americas — some accounts put it on par with Boston. The English Crown had a specific, cynical strategy here: rather than fight privateers and buccaneers, pay them. Port Royal became a licensed base for privateers like Henry Morgan, who was later knighted and made Lieutenant Governor of Jamaica, sanctioned to raid Spanish shipping and ports throughout the Caribbean.

That money had to go somewhere, and contemporary accounts describe a town absolutely stuffed with taverns — one widely cited period estimate puts roughly one drinking house for every ten residents. It also had something rarer for the 1680s Caribbean: real civic infrastructure. Brick townhouses up to four stories tall, proper warehouses, a busy customs house, and a thriving slave market, since Port Royal was also a major hub of the transatlantic slave trade into the English colonies — a history as central to the town's wealth as its piracy, and one worth naming plainly rather than skipping past.

**[2:30–4:30] THE MORNING OF JUNE 7TH, 1692**

(Visual: a clear blue Caribbean morning over the harbor town; then the ground beginning to ripple)

The earthquake hit at 11:38 in the morning, under a clear sky, with no warning. Survivor accounts — several of which were written down and sent back to London within weeks, and which historians still rely on today — describe the ground visibly rippling, like waves moving through solid earth, before it opened.

The Reverend Emmanuel Heath, rector of Port Royal's own church, was one of the people who lived to describe it. He wrote of the earth "heaving and rolling" under his feet, of houses "sinking entirely," and of watching the ground open and swallow people where they stood, some of whom he could see still struggling in the sand and surf. He himself survived by being thrown into a boat.

Modern geological assessment backs up the speed in the accounts: Port Royal was built on loose, sandy, reclaimed land, and that kind of soil is especially vulnerable to a process called liquefaction — a strong enough shock turns saturated sand into something that behaves almost like liquid. The ground didn't crumble or erode. It briefly stopped acting solid, and an estimated two-thirds of the town's buildings, along with the sand spit's shoreline itself, slid downward and outward into the harbor within minutes.

Contemporary death toll estimates vary — some near-contemporary sources cite around 2,000 dead in the earthquake itself, with more lost afterward to the disease that swept through survivor camps in the weeks that followed, on top of the original population of roughly 6,500 to 10,000. The exact numbers aren't precisely fixed in the surviving records, which is typical for a disaster this size in 1692 — but every account agrees on the same core fact: a significant fraction of a major city went under the water in a matter of minutes, not years.

**[4:30–6:40] WHAT'S ACTUALLY DOWN THERE**

(Visual: underwater archaeology footage style — a diver's light passing over a submerged brick wall, cobblestones visible through clear water)

This is the part that makes Port Royal different from most "lost city" stories: it isn't legend, and it isn't buried under centuries of silt where no one can check. It's a short swim offshore in Kingston Harbour, in relatively shallow water, and archaeologists have been diving it since the 1950s.

Texas A&M University's nautical archaeology program ran major systematic excavations at the site starting in the 1980s, led by Dr. Donny Hamilton, and what they mapped was remarkable: intact street grids, still-standing brick building foundations, and household items frozen mid-use — pewter plates, wine bottles, even a pocket watch stopped at the moment it hit the water, which gave archaeologists a startlingly precise time check against the written eyewitness accounts. Because the site sank quickly and was then covered by harbor sediment, it preserved details land excavations of the period rarely do.

The submerged portion of Port Royal is on Jamaica's National Heritage Trust list, and in 2024 UNESCO added it to Jamaica's official Tentative List for World Heritage status — specifically citing it as one of the most completely preserved examples of 17th-century urban archaeology anywhere in the Americas, on land or underwater.

**[6:40–8:00] WHAT HAPPENED TO THE CITY AFTER**

(Visual: present-day Port Royal — a modest fishing village on the same spit, Fort Charles still standing)

Port Royal never fully rebuilt as the dominant city it had been. Fires, a hurricane, and another major earthquake in 1907 each took another piece of what survived in 1692, and the colonial capital and commercial center of gravity shifted to Kingston, across the harbor, which remains Jamaica's capital today.

What's left on the surface now is a small fishing town of a few thousand people, with Fort Charles — one of the few 17th-century structures that survived 1692 relatively intact — still standing as a museum. The wicked, wealthy city underneath it is the one divers actually visit.

**[8:00–8:50] CLOSE / CTA**

Most "sunken city" stories are the kind you can never actually verify — a legend, a guess, a single ambiguous ruin. Port Royal is the opposite: a precisely dated morning, survivor testimony written down within weeks, and an entire street grid that archaeologists can still walk, underwater, today. If finding real, verifiable lost places hiding in plain sight is your kind of thing, the next one's already up.
